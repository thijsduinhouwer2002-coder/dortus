How to read email headers to spot phishing

An employee forwards an email to the service desk: “Is this legit?” The text and the sender name tell you little, because the sender wrote both. The headers are different: the mail servers the email passed through added them, and the server at your end wrote down whether the sender was allowed to use that domain. This page shows where the headers are, how to get them out of Outlook or a saved .msg or .eml file, what to look for, and how this works on a Jira work item.

Published 30 October 2026

What email headers are

Every email starts with a block of header lines that the mail app hides. Some are written by the sender and can say anything: From, To, Subject, Date, Reply-To. Others are written by the servers that handled the email: each adds a Received line, and the server at your end usually adds an Authentication-Results line with the SPF, DKIM and DMARC results. Those are the lines you can trust.

One limit up front: an email that never left your organisation often has no authentication results at all. That is not suspicious in itself.

Where to find the headers

In Outlook

  • Classic Outlook for Windows. Open the email in its own window and choose File → Properties. The headers are in the Internet headers box.
  • New Outlook for Windows. Open the email, click More actions (the three dots) and choose View → View message details (Microsoft: View internet message headers in Outlook).
  • Outlook on the web. Open the email, click the three dots in the reading pane and choose View → View message details.
  • Outlook for Mac. Control-click the email in the list and choose View Source.

In a saved .eml or .msg file

Reported emails often arrive as a file, because the reporter forwarded the email as an attachment. An .eml file is plain text: open it in Notepad or TextEdit and the headers are the first lines, down to the first empty line. A .msg file is Outlook’s binary format: open it in Outlook for Windows and use File → Properties. A .msg only carries internet headers if the email came in over the internet (see Outlook .msg vs .eml). On a Mac without Outlook, see how to open a .msg file on a Mac.

A header analyser such as Microsoft’s Message Header Analyzer or MxToolbox lays the block out as a table, with the hops in order. These tools run on their makers’ servers, so do not paste headers from an email you may not share.

Five things to check

  1. Authentication-Results. Look for spf=, dkim= and dmarc=. pass means the sending server was allowed to send for that domain and the message was not changed on the way. fail is a strong warning. none means the domain publishes no policy, which is common for small senders and says little on its own. Only the line added by your mail server counts; a forwarded email can carry results from the reporter’s server too.
  2. From versus Return-Path. From is what the reader sees. Return-Path is where bounces go, and the address SPF was checked against. A newsletter service legitimately uses a different one. A bank sending from a random domain does not.
  3. Reply-To. If it points to a different domain than From, replies go somewhere else than the reader thinks. Invoice fraud relies on this.
  4. The Received lines. Read them from the bottom up: the lowest line is the first server that handled the email, the top line is your own. A mail from “your IT department” that entered through a consumer mail provider abroad deserves a second look. The very first hop can be forged; the lines your own servers added cannot.
  5. The sender domain. Look for lookalike letters and domains starting with xn--, an international domain name that can hide accented or Cyrillic letters. A typo in plain letters, such as a 1 for an l, you have to spot by eye.

Then look at the body the same way: links whose text names one domain while the address goes to another, attachments that can run code (.exe, .js, .html, .svg, archives with such files inside), and urgency.

Where this falls short

Headers tell you whether the email came from where it claims. They do not tell you whether a genuine but compromised mailbox sent it: phishing from a hacked supplier passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Reading headers also takes time and the wrong tool. A service desk agent working from a Jira work item has to download the .msg, open Outlook, find Properties and paste into an analyser, for every report. On a Mac or a phone, a .msg does not even open.

FAQ

Can email headers be faked?

The lines the sender writes can: From, Reply-To, Subject, Date, and even the first Received line. The lines your own mail server added cannot, including the Authentication-Results line with the SPF, DKIM and DMARC results. Read the headers from your server down.

What does SPF, DKIM or DMARC none mean?

The sending domain publishes no SPF record, no DKIM signature was found, or the domain has no DMARC policy. Many small businesses are in that position, so none is not proof of phishing. Fail is the result to act on.

The email passed SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Is it safe?

It came from a server allowed to send for that domain and was not changed on the way. That is all. A hacked mailbox at a real supplier passes every check, so still look at the Reply-To, the links and the attachments.

Why does my .msg file have no internet headers?

Outlook only stores internet headers in a .msg when the email came in over the internet. An email sent inside the same Exchange organisation never had them. An .eml file carries whatever headers the email had.
