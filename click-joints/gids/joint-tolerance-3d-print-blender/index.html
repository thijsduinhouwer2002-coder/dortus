Joint tolerance for 3D printing in Blender: what clearance to use

A printed joint only moves if there is a small gap between the two parts. Too small and they fuse or jam. Too big and the joint wobbles. This page explains what that gap means in Blender, where to start, and how to find the number that fits your own printer.

Clearance, tolerance, gap: what it means

People use the three words for the same thing: the empty space between a moving part and the part around it. For a ball joint it is the space between the ball and the socket, on each side. With a clearance of 0.20 mm, the socket is 0.40 mm wider than the ball, so 0.20 mm all around.

Your printer does not print that gap exactly. The first layer squishes out, walls come out a little thicker, and resin parts often come out a little oversized. That is why the right clearance is different for every printer, and why you set it in the model, not in the slicer.

Where to start

  • 0.20 mm is a good first try. It is the default in Click Joints.
  • 0.1 to 0.3 mm is the typical range for FDM and resin. A well tuned printer can go lower; a looser one needs more.
  • Small figures need care. On a small part the same gap is a bigger share of the joint. Click Joints works best on figures of about 200 mm or taller.

These are starting points, measured in Blender, not print tested by us yet. Print a test joint before you print the whole figure.

Set the clearance in Blender

By hand, you model the ball, then make the socket from a second sphere that is larger by twice the clearance, and use it as a Boolean difference. It works, but it is easy to be off by a few tenths, and you can not see the gap once the parts are joined.

With Click Joints (a free Blender add-on made by us at Dortus) you type the clearance in mm and the add-on cuts both parts around it:

  1. Install Click Joints. In Blender 4.2 or newer: Edit, Preferences, Get Extensions, search joints, Install. Press N in the 3D viewport and open the Click Joints tab.
  2. Check the model. Click Check mesh. Your model should be in millimetres and watertight; the panel offers Convert to mm and Make watertight (on a copy) if it is not.
  3. Set the clearance. Under 2 Joint, type the clearance, for example 0.20 mm. Leave Diameter on Auto.
  4. Place the joint. Shift + right click to put the 3D cursor on the joint, then click Place joint. You get two watertight parts with that gap between them.
  5. Read the fit ring. A ring on the cut shows the fit: green good, yellow tight, red too thin. The panel gives the wall in mm and the clearance it measured on the parts.

Clearance and wall go together

A bigger gap means a bigger socket, and a bigger socket leaves less material around it. If you raise the clearance, check the wall again. Click Joints marks a wall under 1.5 mm as Tight (yellow) and under 0.8 mm as Too thin (red). With Diameter on Auto it picks the largest ball that still keeps a good wall at that clearance.

Find the number for your printer

The quickest way is to print several clearances at once and pick the best one. Our free fit test strip has five ball joints from 0.10 to 0.30 mm, with the clearance printed on each part. Print it flat end down, no supports, put each pair together and use the clearance of the one that turns smoothly without wobble. Then type that number in Click Joints for your figure.
